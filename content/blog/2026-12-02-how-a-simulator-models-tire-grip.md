A racing simulator is different from an arcade game in one important way. In many arcade games, the car sticks to the road until the game decides it should not. In a simulator, the tires have a limit, and you can use it up. Understanding that limit is the quickest way to stop sliding wide and start getting around the track faster.

## Grip is a limit

Virtual Racing School, a sim racing training site, explains that a tire's grip is limited. The article says there is "a finite limit to the amount of grip or force which can be produced in any direction."

That sentence is the core of this lesson. A tire can only do so much. It does not have one supply of grip for braking, another for turning and another for acceleration. All of that work comes from the same limited supply.

## The traction circle

To show this, the school uses a diagram called the traction circle. The outer ring of the circle represents 100 percent of the grip a tire has available. Each thing you ask the car to do uses part of that grip.

Picture the circle with four directions. Straight up is acceleration, straight down is braking, and left and right are cornering. Any combination of those demands is a point inside the circle. As long as the point stays inside the outer ring, the tire holds. If the combined demand goes past the ring, the tire slides.

The useful part is what this says about combinations. If you use all your grip for braking, there is none left for turning. If you use all of it for turning, there is none left for the throttle.

:::

## What goes wrong at the corner entry

The school gives a clear example. If you brake as hard as the tires allow and then turn in sharply, you ask the front tires for two things at once. The article says this can overload the front tires, causing them to lock and push wide.

Most new sim racers know this feeling. The car does not turn, even though the wheel is turned. The instinct is to turn the wheel more, but that asks for even more grip from tires that are already out of it. The fix is to ask for less.

## The fix: ease off the brake as you turn

Virtual Racing School's recommendation is to ease off the brake as you steer. As you add steering, you take away brake pressure, so the combined demand on the front tires stays inside the circle.

In practice, the early part of the corner is mostly braking. Then, as the car begins to turn, the brake pressure fades while the steering angle builds. By the time you are turning fully, the brake is gone and the tires are using their grip for cornering.

## At the apex and on the way out

The article also covers the middle and end of the corner. At the apex, the point of the corner where you are closest to the inside, the tires should be giving maximum lateral grip, which means sideways grip, with little pedal input. At that moment the car is doing one job, turning, so the tires can put nearly all their grip into it.

After the apex, you start adding throttle gradually. The article says to stay near the edge of the circle until reaching full throttle. As the steering unwinds and the cornering demand drops, there is more grip free for acceleration, so you can add more throttle. If you add it too quickly while still turning, you ask for too much at once and the car slides.

Put together, the corner is a trade. Braking gives way to turning, and turning gives way to accelerating. The total demand stays near the edge of the circle without going over.

## Using this in a session

Here are things you can try the next time you are in a simulator.

Brake in a straight line first. Do your hardest braking before you start to turn, so the tires are not asked to do both.

Release the brake smoothly. Do not let go of it all at once as you turn in. Fade it out while the steering builds.

Be patient with the throttle. Wait until the car is pointed toward the exit before you add much power, then build it gradually.

Watch for the signs of going past the limit. A front end that pushes wide on entry usually means too much brake or too much steering for the grip available. The cure is to ask for less of one of them.

Virtual Racing School also notes that its driving analyzer can display a traction circle. That kind of display shows how well a driver uses the available grip across braking, cornering and acceleration. If the software you use offers a similar display, it can show you where in a lap you are over or under using the tires.

## What to remember

A tire has one limited supply of grip. Braking, cornering and accelerating all take from it, and the traction circle is a way to picture that. Overloading the front tires by braking hard while turning sharply makes the car push wide. Easing off the brake as you steer, getting the car turned at the apex, and adding throttle gradually on the way out all keep the total demand near the edge of the circle without going past it.

That is the idea behind a lot of what makes a simulator feel real, and it is a good first thing to practice.
