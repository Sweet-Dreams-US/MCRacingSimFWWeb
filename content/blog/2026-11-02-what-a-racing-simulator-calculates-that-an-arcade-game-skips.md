If you have only played racing games on a console or a phone, a racing simulator can feel like a different thing that happens to share a name. Both put you in a car on a track. What differs is what the software is trying to do. An arcade game tries to give you the feeling of speed. A simulator tries to copy how a real car behaves. This post explains the difference in plain terms, so you know what to expect before you sit down.

## What "simulator" actually means

Sim racing is an umbrella term for racing games that try to accurately simulate auto racing. According to the Wikipedia article on sim racing, these games model real-world variables such as fuel use, damage, tire wear and grip, and suspension settings. The same article says that competitive play requires understanding real driving techniques, such as threshold braking and keeping the car controlled as the tires lose traction.

That is the core idea. The game is not scripted to make you feel fast. It calculates what the car would do, and you deal with the result.

## What an arcade racer leaves out

Arcade racing games take the opposite approach. They remove many of those real-world variables and focus on a sense of speed rather than realism. Wikipedia uses the Need for Speed series as an example of this style, and notes that by the early 1990s arcade racing put fast-paced action ahead of realistic handling.

Neither style is wrong. An arcade game is built so you can pick up a controller and have fun in a minute. A simulator asks for more attention and gives back more detail. Which one you prefer depends on what you enjoy.

## Tire grip is the heart of it

Early simulators put their effort into tire modeling. Wikipedia describes Live for Speed, released in 2003, as using a brush deflection model for its tires, with later updates adding tire wear, flat spots and tire wall deformation.

Why does this matter to a driver? Because grip is limited, and it is shared. Virtual Racing School describes the traction circle as a way to picture this: there is a finite limit to the amount of grip a tire can produce in any direction. If you use a lot of that grip for braking, there is less left for turning. The same source explains that a driver can use the available grip better by combining braking, steering and acceleration, and that the correct technique is to reduce braking pressure as you begin to steer.

In a simulator this is part of the driving. Brake hard while turning and there is less grip left for the turn, so the car may not turn as much as you expected. Arcade games, which remove many real-world variables, do not ask you to manage this.

:::note What the traction circle says
Virtual Racing School describes the idea this way: "there is a finite limit to the amount of grip or force which can be produced in any direction." Braking, turning and accelerating all draw from the same pool.
:::

## Fuel, damage and suspension

Grip is only one of the variables. A simulator can also track:

Fuel use. Wikipedia names fuel as one of the core simulated variables.

Damage. Live for Speed models car body and engine damage, and Wikipedia says iRacing's 2021 damage model uses soft body physics, which affects how a vehicle behaves after a crash.

Suspension settings. Wikipedia notes that simulators extended their modeling to suspension, aerodynamics and drivetrain parts over time, and that a 1989 title, Indy 500, let players adjust tires, shocks and wings in a garage.

The practical effect is that the car you drive at the end of a session is not the same car you started with. It has used fuel, worn its tires and perhaps taken a hit. A driver who pays attention to that will do better than one who treats every lap the same.

## Why the hardware changes the experience

A simulator is only as convincing as the equipment you drive with. Wikipedia recommends a racing wheel and pedals for immersion, and says gamepads or a keyboard are adequate for arcade-style games but offer less realism. It also says simulators need relatively powerful computers.

Force feedback has been part of racing games for a long time. Wikipedia notes that the 1983 arcade game TX-1 vibrated the steering wheel, and that WEC Le Mans in 1986 used a wheel that responded to acceleration and road bumps. In a simulator, that wheel is one way the car's behavior reaches your hands.

## So which should you try first?

If you want to relax and race with friends, an arcade game does that well. If you are curious about how a car really behaves, or you want a skill you can get better at over many sessions, a simulator is worth an afternoon. Many people enjoy both.

One piece of advice for a simulator: drive smoothly. Smooth inputs ask less of the tires at any one moment, which leaves more grip in reserve.
