In an arcade racing game, you can hit a wall at full speed, bounce off, and keep going as if nothing happened. In a simulator, that same hit can end your race, or leave you driving a car that no longer behaves the way it did a lap ago. The feature behind that difference is called damage modeling.

This post explains what damage modeling is, what it changes about driving, and how to adjust your habits if you are new to sim racing.

## What damage modeling is

Damage modeling is the part of a simulator that decides what happens to the car when it hits something. A simple version only changes how the car looks. A full version changes how the car works.

Wikipedia's overview of sim racing lists damage among the real-world variables that simulators try to reproduce, alongside things like tire wear and suspension. The same article notes that Live for Speed models car body damage and engine damage, and that iRacing introduced a revamped damage model in 2021 that affects vehicle behavior when drivers crash on track.

Arcade racers take a different approach. The same source says they mainly prioritize a sense of speed over a sense of realism. That is why contact in an arcade game costs you so little.

## How a sim builds damage

iRacing's developers wrote about how they approached damage for their simulator in 2018. Their article describes the bodywork of a car as shapes that can deform when hit. Each car also has damage points that track harm in local areas. Repeated contact gradually weakens those points until the affected part crushes or comes off.

The parts that can come off include body panels, hoods, and wings, and wheels can be removed as well. When a part detaches, it takes its mass with it. The developers explained that this lets the simulation shed mass and energy correctly during big crashes.

:::note Worth knowing
iRacing's developers wrote that rebuilding the car's structure lets the physics side of the simulation more accurately represent the effect damage has on a car's handling model. In plain terms, a damaged car is simulated as a different car.
:::

## Why damage changes handling

A race car is a balanced system. Weight, grip, and the shape of the car all work together. When a sim changes one of those through damage, the rest of the car responds differently.

The sources above do not list every handling effect, and the effects differ from one title to another. What they do establish is that damage is part of the physics, not a cosmetic layer on top of it. So the safe way to think about it is this: after contact, assume the car is no longer the car you qualified in.

That has a practical meaning. Your braking points, your turn-in, and your confidence on the throttle were all learned with an undamaged car. A damaged car may not respond the way those habits expect.

## How to drive differently

A few habits help in any sim that models damage.

First, treat contact as a cost, not an event. In an arcade game, a bump is free. In a sim, a bump can end your race, so leave room. Brake a little earlier when you are close to another car, and do not try to pass where there is no room to pass.

Second, learn to read the car after a hit. If the car pulls to one side, feels loose, or sounds wrong, slow down and find out what has changed before you put the next corner under it. Pushing at full pace in a damaged car is how a small mistake becomes a bigger one.

Third, finish. It is tempting to make up lost time right after a hit. A slower, controlled lap in a damaged car usually beats a second crash.

Fourth, check the damage settings in whatever you are racing on, because they can differ between titles and between servers. Know what is switched on before you race, because it changes how much a mistake costs.

## Why this matters for learning

Damage modeling is one reason sim racing teaches real habits. When mistakes cost something, drivers learn to be smooth, to plan ahead, and to give other cars space. Wikipedia describes competitive drivers as needing a close understanding of car handling, including threshold braking and regaining control when tires lose traction. Damage adds one more thing to that list: keeping the car in one piece.

## The short version

Damage modeling means a crash changes the car for the rest of your race. Leave more room, slow down to find out what a hit has changed, and finish the race when you can. Those habits will help you in any sim, and they carry over to the other kinds of racing you try.
