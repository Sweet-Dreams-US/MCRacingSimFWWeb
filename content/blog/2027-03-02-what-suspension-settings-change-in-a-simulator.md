A good racing simulator does more than show you a car on screen. It models how the car moves, and that includes the suspension. In many simulators you can open a garage menu and change settings that alter how the car grips, turns and settles over bumps. If you have never touched those menus, they can look like a wall of numbers. This guide explains what each main setting does in plain terms.

## Why suspension settings matter in a simulator

A simulator that models the car properly is different from an arcade game, where the car is mostly a shape that goes where you point it. In a simulator, weight moves around the car as you brake, accelerate and turn, and the suspension controls how that happens. Changing a setting changes the balance of the car.

The sources used for this post come from sim racing setup guides. Suspension Secrets stresses a useful habit: make small changes, one at a time, so you can tell which change helped.

## Springs

Springs hold the car up and decide how much it moves when weight shifts. Suspension Secrets notes that softer springs generally add grip, since they reduce lateral load transfer and absorb bumps. The LFS Manual describes the other side: stiffer springs give faster chassis movements but reduce the amount of travel of the tire.

Both go wrong at the extremes. Suspension Secrets warns that if the spring is made too soft, the car can bottom out, which slows it down. If you run the car too stiff, it will begin to skip and hop over bumps, losing grip and making the car slower.

:::note Worth knowing
Soft is not always better and stiff is not always better. Springs are a trade between grip over bumps and how quickly the car responds.
:::

## Dampers

Dampers, also called shock absorbers, control how fast the suspension moves. Bump damping controls how quickly the damper compresses. Rebound damping controls how quickly it extends. A stiffer setting resists the movement and a softer setting allows it.

The LFS Manual says the softer you make a shock absorber, the faster the weight is transferred. That makes dampers a way to change how quickly the car reacts when you turn in or lift off the throttle. Suspension Secrets adds a caution about the rear: a higher value at the rear wheels will make the car less stable under braking.

## Anti-roll bars

Anti-roll bars resist body roll in corners. The LFS Manual says a properly selected anti-roll bar will reduce body roll in corners for improved cornering traction. Suspension Secrets explains that these bars work only in cornering, and that the stiffness at each end of the car changes its balance. A stiffer front bar tends to increase understeer, which is when the front of the car pushes wide. A stiffer rear bar tends to increase oversteer, which is when the rear steps out.

If an anti-roll bar is set too stiff, Suspension Secrets warns that too much lateral load transfer will occur.

## Ride height

Ride height is how far the chassis sits above the ground. The LFS Manual says a lower setting will lower the centre of gravity, which helps overall handling. The cost is risk. Go too low and the car can bottom out on kerbs and bumps. Suspension Secrets makes the same point and says the front and rear can be set separately, which creates a rake angle.

## Camber

Camber is the tilt of the wheel when you look at the car from the front. Suspension Secrets says negative camber, where the top of the wheel leans inward, is standard on race and track cars for cornering grip. The LFS Manual says negative camber helps the outside tires keep good contact in corners, but a lot of camber will also reduce braking and accelerating abilities.

Suspension Secrets also warns that too much camber can make the tire overheat on the inside edge and break down.

## Toe

Toe is the angle of the wheels when you look at the car from above. Toe in points the front of the wheels toward the car's centre, and toe out points them away. The LFS Manual says toe in will get you more stability, while toe out will get you more turn in but make the car more nervous.

Suspension Secrets gives the same pattern. Front toe out sharpens turn in and reduces understeer but can make straight line running feel unstable, and rear toe in adds stability and reduces oversteer on corner entry. Suspension Secrets also warns that toe in on the front or rear wheels will increase tire temperatures because of the tire scrubbing.

## How to start tuning

Start with one change at a time and drive a few laps before you decide. If you change springs, dampers and anti-roll bars together, you will not know which one made the car better or worse. Suspension Secrets recommends the same approach.

Pick a car and a track you already know. Note how the car feels first. Then change one setting by a small amount, drive again and compare.

## What to take away

Each setting has a job. Springs and dampers decide how the car moves over bumps and how fast weight shifts. Anti-roll bars decide how the car leans in corners. Ride height, camber and toe set how the tires meet the road. Every setting has a cost at the extremes, which is why small steps work better than big ones.
